1. Greetings

食飽未 (tsia̍h-pá--buē) literally means "have you eaten?". In Taiwanese, people often use 食飽未 (tsia̍h-pá--buē) to show their concern to others. It has basically the same meaning as "what's up?" or "how are you?"

There are many ways to respond. You can say 食飽矣 (tsia̍h-pá--ah), meaning that I have eaten, or 有喔 (ū--ooh) meaning yeah. You don't even necessarily have to repond but asking 食飽未 (tsia̍h-pá--buē) back!

2. Personal pronouns

The following are the personal pronouns in Taiwanese:

Taiwanese Tailo English
我 guá I. me
你 lí you (singular)
伊 i he/she/it, him/her/it
阮 gún we (excluding listener)
咱 lán we (including listener)
恁 lín you (plural), you (formal)
𪜶 in they, them

As you might notice, the same word in Taiwanese can mean both the subject and the object pronoun. This is because the concepts cases doesn't really exist in Taiwanese. Whether a noun is subject or object can only be known by its position in a sentence. For instance, 我愛你 (Guá ài lí, I love you.), since 我 (guá, I/me) comes before the verb 愛 (ài, to love), it is a subject; since 你 (guá, you (single)) comes before the verb 愛 (ài, to love), it is an object.

Another thing that's worth noting is the difference between 阮 (gún) and 咱 (lán). Both are translated into we in English. 阮 (gún) excludes the listener. For example, if I want to tell you that my another friend and I are Taiwanese, I can say 阮是台灣人 (Gún sī Tâi-uân-lâng, we are Taiwanese). 咱 (lán), on the other hand, includes the listener, so 咱是台灣人 (Gún sī Tâi-uân-lâng) means that you and I (might or might not include other people) are Taiwanese.

3. Verb 是

The verb 是 is used to explain that the thing before it and sfter it are equal. It similar to the verb to be when it is a noun coming after the be verb. For example, 這是狗 (Tse sī káu) means that 這 (guá, this), and 狗 (káu, dog) are equal, that is, "This is a dog."
